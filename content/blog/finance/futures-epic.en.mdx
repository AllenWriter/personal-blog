Have you ever asked yourself this?

If futures are meant to **reduce risk**, why do they always show up in the news in such a bloody, dramatic, almost casino-like way?

An 8-minute trading session in a national bond futures contract brought down a brokerage. A nickel price that doubled twice in two days scared the world's biggest nickel trader and JPMorgan half to death. Crude oil futures fell below zero, which meant "I'll give you the oil for free and pay you to take it."

You'd find that strange.

If a financial instrument was really created to help the real economy manage risk, why does everyone remember it only for:

- Short squeezes
- Forced liquidations
- Negative equity
- Manipulation
- Margin calls
- Trades being voided by the exchange
- Institutions going bankrupt
- Retail investors buying the dip all the way to the basement

Doesn't something seem off here?

The problem is, it isn't wrong, either.

Futures weren't invented by a casino. They aren't a demon toy Wall Street dreamed up with nothing better to do. At heart, they're one of the cleverest, most advanced, and most necessary inventions in human commercial history.

It's just that this thing is too powerful.

How powerful?

Powerful enough that once it gets tangled up with leverage, delivery, liquidity, funding pressure, and human greed and fear, it can turn from a rational risk-management tool into a super-amplifier that flips over the entire market mood, an entire financial institution, and even the whole regulatory system.

If you forced me to sum up futures in one sentence, I'd say:

**Futures aren't for trading commodities. What futures really trade is "uncertainty about the future."**

And the most expensive thing in human history has always been uncertainty.

## I. What is a futures contract, really? It looks like it trades goods, but it's really trading "tomorrow"

Let's make this as simple as possible.

Say you run a milk tea shop. What do you fear most?

You fear rising ingredient costs. Milk, tea leaves, fruit, sugar. Today the costs look perfectly normal. Then three months from now, prices jump, and the profit on every cup you sell disappears.

So what do you do?

A very down-to-earth solution is this: you agree with your supplier in advance. "In three months, I'll buy a batch from you at today's price."

That is essentially the original logic behind futures.

Now flip it around.

Say you're a soybean farmer. What you fear isn't rising prices. What you fear is a bumper harvest that sends prices crashing. You've worked hard all year, and when the harvest comes in, supply floods the market and the price collapses. So what do you do?

You'd think: can I lock in the future selling price ahead of time?

See? One person fears the future will be too expensive. The other fears it will be too cheap.

One wants to buy in advance. The other wants to sell in advance.

As soon as they agree on that price ahead of time, a futures contract is born.

So futures started out very simply. They weren't for speculation. They were for:

**Giving the future a price first.**

How important is that?

Extremely important.

Because what the real economy fears most isn't earning a little less money. It's having no way to plan for the future at all.

If an airline doesn't know whether oil prices will spike, how does it set ticket prices? If a bakery doesn't know whether wheat will surge, how can it sign next year's supply contracts? If a metals plant doesn't know how copper, nickel, or aluminum will move next year, how can it accept long-term orders?

So you'll find that the greatest thing about futures markets has never been "making people rich." It's this:

**They let society bundle up part of the uncertainty about the future, price it in advance, and transfer it ahead of time.**

That is their real civilizational value.

## II. The trouble is, once humans can trade the future, they'll inevitably start amplifying it

Up to this point, futures sound pretty righteous, right?

Very reasonable. Very down-to-earth. Very productive.

But here's the problem: anything that can be traded will inevitably attract people who aren't satisfied with using it just to hedge. They start thinking:

**Can I use this to make even more money?**

That's close to an iron law of financial history.

At first, people used it to lock in prices. Then they realized:

Hey, I don't actually need to own the goods. I don't need to make delivery. As long as I can judge where future prices are heading, I can place my bets in advance.

See, that's when things start to change.

Futures slowly transform from a "risk transfer tool between producers and buyers" into:

- A hedging tool
- An arbitrage tool
- A speculation tool
- A leverage tool
- A gambling tool

It's no longer just "an agreement between a farmer and a factory." It becomes a vast arena for betting on future prices.

And once you reach that point, the most seductive and the most dangerous qualities of futures show up at the same time:

### It's very capital-efficient

You don't need to pay the full value of the goods. You only need to post a portion as margin.

### It's very efficient

A small move in price gets magnified into your profit or loss very quickly.

### It's very transparent

The market quotes you a price every single day.

### It's brutally unforgiving

Because if the price moves against you even a little, you may be asked to top up your margin right away. If you can't, sorry, your position gets closed out.

So what are futures most like?

They're like a highly advanced expressway system. They were built to make freight more efficient and keep society running smoothly. But once cars go faster, traffic gets heavier, and drivers get overexcited with no strict rules or brakes, the road turns into a pile-up.

Futures are the same.

They aren't evil by nature. They're just too fast. Too sensitive. Too good at amplifying.

And anything that magnifies "uncertainty about the future" many times over ultimately forces you to face one fact:

> What's truly terrifying about financial markets isn't that prices are wrong. It's that you run out of money before prices correct.

## III. Why do futures drive people crazy? Because what they trade isn't "right or wrong" but "can you survive until the end?"

This point is crucial.

Many people understand markets as:

- I'm bullish, because I think it should go up
- You're bearish, because you think it should go down
- Whoever's right in the end makes money

That sounds fine. But in futures, it's only half the story.

The brutal part of the futures market is this:

**Being right doesn't mean you'll survive long enough to get paid.**

This logic is extremely important.

Say you're short. You think this asset is overvalued and will eventually fall. Over the long run, you may be completely correct.

But if it rallies sharply before it actually falls, and you're using high leverage, you may get forcibly liquidated by the exchange "before the truth arrives."

By the time it really falls, you're already gone from the table.

That's why the scariest thing in futures isn't "being wrong." It's this:

**Even if you're right, you can still die along the way.**

So in many cases, the futures market isn't really a contest of logic. It's a contest of:

- Capital
- Leverage
- Liquidity
- Margin
- The structure of counterparties
- Delivery constraints
- And whether you can hang on until that final moment

Put simply:

**Futures are not about who understands the future better. Very often, they're about who doesn't blow up before the future actually arrives.**

That's why so many classic futures battles look so much like wars.

Because the cruelest thing in war isn't "who has a point." It's "who can survive to the last round."

## IV. The 327 Treasury Bond Futures: 8 minutes, how did they knock a giant to its knees?

There's a famous story in Chinese financial history called the **327 Incident**.

Why is it so famous?

Because it showcased nearly every dangerous element of futures all at once:

- High leverage
- Giants betting against each other
- Disagreement over fundamentals
- Short squeezes
- Market manipulation
- The exchange forcibly voiding trades
- A financial institution nearly getting wiped out
- The regulatory system being forced to reshape itself

Let's start with the background.

In 1995, there was a national bond futures contract code-named **327**. On the surface, it was just an ordinary government bond futures contract. But the problem was that the Ministry of Finance had a policy of inflation-linked compensation for government bonds, and nobody in the market fully knew how big that compensation would be.

That meant a big gap in expectations sat underneath the price.

Some people thought the compensation would be generous, so bonds would be worth more and prices should rise. Others thought the compensation had already peaked, the outlook wasn't so rosy, and prices should fall.

So bulls and bears started betting against each other.

On the long side was mainly **China Economic Development Trust (中经开)**. On the short side was **Wanguo Securities** together with **Liaoning Guofa**.

At first, it still looked like a normal battle between big institutions. Wanguo believed it was right, so it kept adding to its short position. From 400,000 contracts, to 800,000, to 1.4 million.

You can see where the danger comes in.

If you only express a view with a small position, you can accept it if you're wrong. But once your position is big enough that it starts tying the company's fate to the outcome, you're no longer betting on a view. You're pledging the entire balance sheet of your institution to back your opinion.

Then what happened next is the classic death spiral of futures:

1. Fundamentals turn against you
2. Prices start moving the opposite way
3. Your paper losses grow
4. You refuse to admit defeat and keep adding to your position
5. Your position gets bigger, and your margin for error gets smaller
6. One more small price move is enough to cause serious damage

Here's a deeply counterintuitive point:

Bond futures prices don't usually move that much. You won't see them swing tens of percentage points in a day the way Bitcoin can. But at the time, people were using **40x leverage**.

So the price move wasn't simply "a dollar up." For the short side, a one-dollar rise could be enough to make the company throw up blood.

Finally came February 23, 1995, the decisive day.

Wanguo found itself cornered. Closing its positions normally would mean admitting defeat. And if it admitted defeat, prices might be pushed even higher. So it took one of the most reckless steps in financial history:

**In the 8 minutes before the close, it dumped a massive amount of short positions.**

It wasn't trying to trade normally anymore. It was betting that:

- If it could push the price down first
- Break the margin chain of its opponents
- Squeeze the longs
- Force them to be liquidated
- Then it could slip out, or even turn the tables

This was no longer "price discovery." It was naked capital combat.

In those 8 minutes, Wanguo dumped an astronomical number of short contracts. The price was driven down from 151.3 all the way to 147.4. If the settlement had been based on that price, Wanguo would have almost pulled off a comeback.

But doing that was too obviously against the rules. That same evening, the exchange announced:

**All the abnormal trades in those last 8 minutes are void.**

The closing price was restored. In one moment, Wanguo went from nearly turning the tables to facing huge losses.

That wasn't just painful. It was a knockout.

Wanguo later came close to bankruptcy, the person in charge was sentenced to prison, and China's national bond futures pilot was suspended for nearly 20 years.

What's the most frightening part of this story?

It isn't that one side was evil. It's that the natural amplifying mechanism of futures, once it meets:

- Giants adding leverage
- Disagreement over fundamentals
- Expiry approaching
- A fragile counterparty
- Games played at the edge of the rules

...quickly upgrades "normal trading" into "you die or I die."

## V. The nickel short squeeze: when the market has only 100 milk teas, but someone has sold 1,500 cups

If the 327 incident is the classic of Chinese-style capital bloodletting, then the 2022 **nickel short squeeze** was a very modern, very dramatic, and very absurd showpiece in the global futures market.

Let's start with a simple version of the logic.

Imagine the market really only has 100 cups of milk tea. But someone has sold futures contracts for 1,500 cups. Do you think that could cause a problem?

In theory, it's usually fine. Most people don't actually need delivery, and many close their positions before expiry. But if something suddenly shocks the market and everyone realizes:

**Oh no, there really aren't enough physical goods available for delivery.**

Then trouble starts.

In 2022, the Russia-Ukraine war broke out. Russia, a major nickel producer, was hit with sanctions. The market suddenly realized:

- Deliverable nickel had become scarcer
- Spot inventories were already low
- But short positions in futures were enormous

So how would the long-side capital that spotted this opportunity think?

It would say:

**You're short a lot, right? Great, then I'll keep buying and push the price up. Either you post more margin, or you close out and admit defeat.**

Qingshan Holdings happened to be in exactly that position, and became the most typical and most conspicuous big short.

Qingshan is a giant in the global nickel industry. Its short logic might not have been purely speculative; it may have included hedging. The question isn't what its motives were. The problem is that it was placed in an extremely dangerous spot:

- Its short position was huge
- Deliverable spot in the market was tiny
- Geopolitics suddenly changed supply
- Longs smelled opportunity and began concentrating to squeeze

The result: nickel prices went wild.

First they doubled. Then doubled again. Finally, they almost shot up like a rocket.

Think about how frightening that is.

For the short side, rising prices weren't just "being wrong about the view." Margin calls kept coming like a bottomless pit. Billions of dollars in funding pressure didn't mean you were fine just because you had assets on paper. You had to produce the cash right now, immediately.

At that point, the thing a short fears most isn't how much they'll lose in the future. It's this:

**I'll run out of cash before tonight.**

Eventually, the London Metal Exchange (LME) stepped in, halted trading, and voided many of that day's trades.

That move blew the market apart. Long-side funds were furious, because they believed they had won by the rules, and then the exchange flipped the table.

Meanwhile, on the short side, Qingshan and the financial chain behind it were effectively propped up by the exchange and the system together.

That's why this episode is so classic. It reveals one of the core truths of futures markets:

**On the surface, futures trading is a price game. At its core, it's a three-way contest between delivery capability, funding capability, and control over the rules.**

Looking only at right and wrong isn't enough. You also need to ask:

- Who can withstand margin calls?
- Who actually has the goods?
- Who gets rescued first by the system when the rules break down?

At that point, this is no longer just a market. It's a power game at the level of financial infrastructure.

## VI. Negative oil prices: how did "we'll give you the oil for free and pay you to take it" happen?

Many people, hearing about negative oil prices for the first time, think it's a joke.

How could oil be negative? Give oil away and pay people to take it? Isn't that crazy?

The truth is, it isn't crazy. It's simply the cruel delivery logic of futures markets pushed to the extreme.

Let's start with the most important point:

**Crude oil futures aren't abstract numbers. At expiry, they require physical delivery.**

In other words, if you hold a long position and don't close it, in theory you really will have to take the oil.

Now the problem: in 2020, when the pandemic broke out, global crude oil demand fell off a cliff. Planes stopped flying. Cars stopped driving. Large parts of industrial activity shut down.

Demand collapsed. Supply couldn't be cut immediately. So the scariest problem in the entire market wasn't that "oil is too cheap." It was:

**There was nowhere to put the oil.**

Storage tanks are finite. Transportation is finite. Delivery locations are finite. You can't just say, "Send me a truckload of oil, and I'll dump it in a river." That's illegal.

Meanwhile, the market was full of longs entering through various "paper oil," "wealth management products," and "agent channels." Many of these people weren't there to take delivery. They just wanted to buy the dip in oil prices.

The problem is, they could buy long, but they had absolutely no ability to take delivery.

So, as expiry approached, the market saw the most terrifying scene:

- Longs had to sell
- Because if they didn't, they'd have to take the oil
- But no one in the market wanted to take it
- Because even if they did, they had nowhere to put it

You can see how the price logic changes at that point.

It's no longer "what is oil worth?" It becomes:

**Who is willing to take this headache off my hands?**

So negative oil prices don't mean that oil itself has no value. They mean:

**At that moment, in that place, under that delivery structure, "getting rid of this long position" was worth more than the oil itself.**

What's that like?

It's like suddenly finding dozens of tons of ice cream in your home. You thought you'd hit the jackpot. Then you realize you don't have a freezer, no logistics, no buyers, and if you don't get rid of it, it all melts. At that point, you're not thinking "how much is this ice cream worth?" You're thinking "who will hurry up and haul it away for me? I'll even pay them."

That's essentially the logic of negative oil prices, magnified to textbook level in the world's most important crude oil futures market.

## VII. So are futures a casino, or a risk management tool?

By this point, you might easily conclude:

Futures are so sinister. So ruthless. So much like a casino.

But if you stop there, you've misunderstood them.

Because the most classic, most thrilling, most life-or-death battles are precisely the most extreme, most out-of-control, and most easily spread side of futures.

They're gripping because they look like accidents.

And accidents are the most attention-grabbing. But you can't say the essence of the road system is crashing just because you watch car accident news every day.

The same goes for futures markets.

The part that's truly huge, truly everyday, and truly supports the real economy isn't these epic crashes. It's countless risk transfers that don't seem that exciting, but are extremely important.

For example:

- Airlines locking in fuel prices
- Farmers locking in grain prices
- Metals plants locking in raw material costs
- Insurers and asset managers using government bond futures to adjust duration risk
- Arbitrage funds trading the basis between spot and futures
- Speculative capital pushing mispricing back to reasonable levels

If you look at a single trade, futures are obviously zero-sum. You win, I lose. I win, you lose.

But if you look at society as a whole, futures aren't just zero-sum.

Because they transfer risk away from "people who can't afford to bear it" and onto "people who are more willing, more professional, and better able to bear it."

A bakery doesn't want to bear the risk of violent swings in wheat prices. So it can sell that risk through futures. Who takes it? Maybe a fund, maybe an arbitrageur, maybe someone willing to bet on prices.

Are these people just speculating?

To some extent, yes. But from a systemic perspective, they're also doing something very important:

**They're bearing and pricing risk on behalf of the entire market.**

That's the subtle thing about futures.

On one hand, they're very much like a casino, because they really do amplify human nature, leverage, fear, and greed to the point of distortion. But on the other hand, they're a huge risk market that the modern economy can't do without.

You can't live without them. Yet once you have them, you must be extremely afraid of them.

---

## Conclusion: futures are most like high-voltage electricity, not a casino

So if you ask me today what futures really are,

I won't say they're a casino. Nor will I say they're "just an ordinary financial tool."

I'd rather compare them to:

**High-voltage electricity.**

Why?

Because high-voltage electricity isn't evil in itself. It's extremely efficient, extremely necessary, and extremely powerful. Without it, much of modern industrial society simply couldn't run.

But the danger is real too.

If you stay too far away from it, society goes dark. If you get too close, you'll be electrocuted on the spot.

So a truly mature market is never one that says "no high-voltage electricity." It's one that:

- Admits it's powerful
- Admits it's dangerous
- Equips it with strict rules, clearing systems, risk controls, delivery mechanisms, and regulatory systems

Because the scariest thing about futures isn't that someone made a lot of money, or that someone lost a lot of money. The scariest thing is when a tool designed to manage risk ends up becoming the risk itself.

And the most classic, bloodiest, most dramatic moments in financial history all remind us of the same thing:

> Humans invented futures to distribute the uncertainty of the future in advance. But once you forget that what they face is fundamentally "uncertainty," they will turn around and teach you a lesson in the most extreme way possible.

So when you look back at the famous squeezes, forced liquidations, negative oil prices, the nickel war, and the 327 incident, you'll see they're about far more than finance.

They're about a bigger question:

**When a system is powerful enough to manage risk, it is also powerful enough to create disaster.**

That is the real epic of futures.
