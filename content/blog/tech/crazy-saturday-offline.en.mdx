## Opening: What Should We Care About in the AI Era?

It's a Saturday afternoon. In a café in Shanghai, a group of young people sits together, ready to start a conversation about AI, investing, and independent development. Some are developers who just moved from the securities industry to a big tech company. Some are college seniors trying to build their first iOS app with AI tools. And some are product managers and founders who are curious about AI agent tools like OpenCloud.

Over three-plus hours, the talk starts with the philosophical questions of AI safety, moves through the underlying logic of US stock investing, and finally lands on a real case of indie iOS development. It's not just a tech talk. It feels more like a collective exploration of how we should think and act in an era that changes so fast.

## Part 1: Philosophical Reflections on OpenCloud and AI Safety

**Speaker:** Steven

**Time:** 00:34:05 - 00:42:48

### Starting from a Real Case

Steven turned on the projector and first shared a real case he had just seen on social media:

"I want to start with privacy and security. Earlier, Eric mentioned that if you deploy an on-device model yourself, that would solve OpenCloud's security problems. But I don't think that's really true."

He described a situation that shocked everyone: "I just saw someone on social media who was using OpenCloud. He uploaded his entire AI configuration, including the API token and all sorts of encryption settings, straight to GitHub, publicly. His API token leaked, just like that."

Someone in the room gasped. Steven went on: "So you see, even if the model runs on-device, you still can't fully control what it does. You can repeatedly write 'don't upload sensitive information' in every skill doc and every system prompt, but the generative nature of AI models means their memory can get tangled, and they can do all kinds of uncontrollable things."

### The Uncontrollability of Technology and the Barrier to Entry

Steven then explained the contradiction in tools like OpenCloud:

"OpenCloud is actually expensive and hard to use. Even if you can deploy it on Docker or a VPS, if you do, you lose its most powerful ability, which is operating your browser, handling your files, and sending WeChat messages for you. But if you deploy it on your own computer, it could delete your files or share your private information at any moment."

"This is because, at the code level and given how uncontrollable AI generative models are, there's no way to completely rule this out at this early stage. That's why everyone is buying Mac Minis." (Laughter in the room.)

### Do We Really Care About Security?

But the discussion quickly turned to a deeper question. Steven paused and said:

"But you know what? I think Chinese people (老中) don't really care that much about data security. That's the first point. The second point: if today were the last day you were alive in this world, and you were asking whether this product is good, you wouldn't think about security at all. You'd only think about what it can actually do."

This hypothetical made the room go quiet for a few seconds. Steven continued:

"Based on these two points, I think the way we're discussing this, as a group like us, carries a huge elitist bias. Because you could do something, the kind of thing Indian founders might do: use a crawler to scrape all of OpenCloud's GitHub issues, find the common problems clustered in those issues, and look for market opportunities or build a better project."

"But doing that... how should I put it? I wouldn't call it stupid, but it isn't that smart. The real problem is this: the people who know about OpenCloud but aren't using it, their problems are the real problems. The people filing issues on GitHub are only a very small part of the picture."

### The Market Education Value of OpenCloud

Steven raised an interesting point:

"OpenCloud now has 170K stars. Out of those 170K stars, something might emerge in April or August of 2026 that's better than it, or that fixes some of its flaws. But the bigger question is why half of the people are still unaware of it. (He meant the potential user market.)"

"So I think it contributes a lot. Just like how it helped everyone order bubble tea earlier, it's doing market education, showing people what the future looks like. It may only be a transitional state, but more products will grow out of it at some stage in the future and solve the concerns people have today."

### OpenCloud Still Fundamentally Depends on the Model

Steven offered a sharp judgment:

"How amazing is it, really? If you swap it onto a really terrible model, say Kimi 1.5, or the models from early 2024, you'll find it isn't magical at all. Its ability to complete tasks might not even match the basic research-level thinking mode OpenAI had back then."

He used a metaphor: "So OpenCloud is most likely just a shell around the model, like a car's body. Whether it's impressive still depends on its battery and its engine, meaning the underlying model."

### Optimism from a Long-Term View

Near the end of the discussion, Steven reached a relatively optimistic conclusion:

"Most of the problems we worry about now probably won't exist in the future. Data issues will persist, that's certain. But I don't think people will develop that deep a sense of security awareness. The people in this room might, but the people outside it won't necessarily. Our elders and our children probably won't."

"And besides, fully controlling AI behavior is also very hard. We want the model, our 'kids,' to follow exactly the instructions I give them, but they can make mistakes, and they'll emerge. They'll do things we didn't expect."

He paused, then stated a more radical view:

"Many parents don't want their kids to play games. They may have a strong desire for control. But that social attitude will shift to some degree over time. I think we have to allow for this deviation. Even from an accelerationist standpoint, we may have to accept that we ourselves will no longer be the center of this society."

"The relationship between humans and AI may slowly move from today's 'ten to zero' to 'eight to two,' or even 'nine to one.' That slider will keep moving. I think this is a reality we have to accept."

## Part 2: OpenCloud's Productization and Its Capability Boundaries

**Discussion time:** 00:40:06 - 00:47:25

**Participants:** Steven, community members

### The Core Problem of Productization

A classmate working in product and business raised a very practical question:

"Many of us who work in product and business care a lot about where OpenCloud's capability boundaries lie. If it's truly productized, how will ordinary people use it?"

He described a concrete pain point: "When we chat with ChatGPT or Claude, we have to explicitly say 'use such-and-such skill for me.' But ordinary users express themselves as 'I want to get something done,' and they expect the AI to call the right skills on its own. So I'm wondering whether it has really done a good job on intent recognition. That's the first problem."

"The second problem is that even for very simple questions, it still tries to call skills. There's a lot of thinking involved, and the cost of those calls adds up, eating a lot of tokens. For that problem, it's not the optimal path. It might even be worse than just using a large model directly."

He finally asked: "Is there a way to optimize its way of thinking, or its calling path, so it makes real progress on token consumption and intent recognition? That way, we could use it in general, everyday scenarios too."

### Stretch the Timeline and None of This Matters

After hearing this, Steven gave an answer that surprised many people:

"I think that's just how it looks from where you're standing right now. If you stretch the timeline infinitely, you'll find that none of this matters."

He gave an example: "Think back to 2023, the GPT-3 era, when the context window was only a few dozen tokens. I forget the exact number, but it was very small. Looking at it now, it doesn't seem like such a big deal. Even whether RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) needs to exist has become unimportant."

"What I mean is that as capabilities keep being unlocked, you can just analyze a big chunk all at once without worrying about token consumption, which is a very minor detail. So I still think it depends on the car engine and the car battery, using the metaphor I mentioned, and on their iteration. As for that framework, which is like the car's shell, it's... not that important."

"The framework-level problems we have right now matter less than model iteration."

### A Three-Factor Framework in Practice

Another participant added that they had recently been testing OpenCloud's limits and had summed up three factors:

**First, the choice of model.**

"A friend of mine told me, 'I installed OpenCloud too, but it feels dumb and hard to use.' So I asked which model he was using, and he said Pinyin, a weak model. Meanwhile, I've been using Claude 4.5 the whole time, now 4.6, and the experience is completely different. **The model is the most important thing.**"

**Second, how clean the context is.**

"OpenCloud's design seems to make you forget that 'context' is even a concept. Unlike ChatGPT or other tools, there's no explicit 'new chat' button. But given what current models can do, starting a new conversation is still very important."

"In OpenCloud, I personally use two commands very often: **`neo` (reset the context) and `C` (compress the context)**. `neo` flips the page and resets the context. `C` compresses the context and rewrites it. This helps it understand your next task better. If you're using very little of the context window, your next command will run a little better."

**Third, prompt design.**

"Prompt design has three layers:

- **System prompt**: defines what kind of assistant the AI is and what role it plays. It's loaded in every conversation.
- **User prompt**: the user's command, which decisively defines the task and the strategy and approach to take.
- **Memory**: not necessarily loaded every time. Memory is a database the model chooses whether to search."

"Together, these three pieces are what bring OpenCloud to the state you want and make it solve problems in a suitable way. So these three are quite important."

## Part 3: Will AI Tools Make Hardware More Expensive?

**Discussion time:** 00:45:18 - 00:52:17

### An Interesting Economics Question

Someone raised a concern about the hardware market:

"I'd like to ask a question that's more about consumers. Do you think tools like OpenCloud could cause the kind of price swings we're seeing with memory today, for Mac Minis or other machines that host this service? If the hardware price swings caused by AI really spread into the personal computer market, what would that look like?"

His logic was: "Say my SaaS product suddenly sells really well. Won't the hardware it runs on go up in price too?"

### The Real Share of the Personal Market

Steven offered a figure that surprised people:

"I think this is a very interesting question. But if you analyze it through economics, **it doesn't mean that just because something sells well, its price goes up.**"

"After that three-hour session (meaning a certain AI product) went viral, the resources on Google Cloud and Amazon Cloud all tilted toward it. After that tilt, there's something in economics called the 'concentration effect,' and it won't become more expensive."

He explained further: "Why? Because what gets the most expensive is **one person's demand**. The demand from 100 people, 10,000 people, or 100,000 people actually goes down. Of course, if the demand you're meeting has to go through chips, computing power, or rare elements constrained by the laws of physics, then it could be expensive. But if it's something current capacity can already supply, it's not necessarily expensive, and it may keep getting cheaper."

Then he laid out a key premise:

"**Personal market demand, compared with data center and office business demand, may not even reach 10%.** That's the simple premise. Given that premise, personal demand only affects the retail price ceiling. In other words, what price you can buy at depends on which channels and sales outlets you can reach."

"For example, you might buy on JD.com, but JD's supply is limited, or Apple's share of sales is limited, so within the existing pool of resources, the price could go up. But don't worry, **supply will naturally catch up**. If demand isn't especially urgent, then with our current supply capacity, you can get as much as you need."

### The Truth About Rising Memory Prices

Someone pressed further: "Then why are memory prices going up?"

Steven gave a very clear answer:

"Memory prices are going up because **Micron switched its capacity, the capacity that used to serve personal PCs, entirely over to data centers and to big customers like NVIDIA.**"

"So the reason manufacturers don't want to expand is that they have to judge whether this demand is short-term or long-term. That's why there will be shortages in the short run. But don't worry. Given Apple's expansion capability, **the supply capacity of all Apple devices is tied to China's industrial capacity**, so it can supply whatever it wants. At most it affects some channel prices. As for retail prices, you might wait two weeks at most."

### OpenCloud's Barrier to Entry Is Still High

Steven summed up at the end:

"And honestly, **OpenCloud's barrier to entry is still very high.** You can't get everything done with a single sentence. This market is nowhere near the level of 'everyone uses it.' So I think the impact on hardware demand is negligible."

"The bigger forces are still changes in enterprise and data center demand. The personal market? If more people knew about this thing... but honestly, our circle is very 'involuted' (卷). In China, the undergraduate education rate is only 4 to 5 percent, and the quality of that is even lower. I don't think many people have this need."

He offered an analogy: "Today everyone is grabbing red envelopes, fighting over 1,000 red envelopes worth 2.5 yuan each, and more people will go for those. But most people really don't... If you affect the existing needs of most people, those needs aren't as rigid as the need to grab train tickets during Spring Festival travel."

The room erupted in laughter.

"Really, what we call growth or making money, I'd like to return to a most fundamental question: **what is money, at its core?**"

"In fact, it didn't. Because there's a basic judgment: a crash just means someone sold, and someone turned the 'goods,' the stock, into cash. The money all left, so the price of the stock fell. **It's really just a transfer. The money wasn't burned. It just moved from one place to another.**"

"It may have pulled out of tech and software, and gone into real estate, bonds, and many other places. Only: **do you know where it went?** That's what matters."

"If you're just an ordinary small retail investor, a leek, you see the drop and can only curse a few times: 'It's down again, when will I get out of this?' And then you just wait. But I think we can think a step further. It's really only when you lose a lot that you start to reflect, to rethink."

"Why do we say finance, the internet, and high-paying industries make money? Because resources are concentrated there. The money isn't sitting in someone's pocket or in a current account. ### The Mathematical Model of Growth

Evan offered a technical yardstick:

"If we want to use tools and have AI understand what I intend, say, 'pick ten stocks that will grow for me,' it will certainly give you something. "A basic concept I really like using now is: **how do you measure whether something is growing all the time?** It's actually just a very simple derivative from high school math. **As long as dt is greater than zero, meaning in the positive interval, you won't have much trouble.**"

"That means someone is continuously investing in it, continuously throwing money into it. Naturally, we would think AI, or some hot industries, or gold, are essentially growth assets."

"So whether it's a zero-sum game or some other kind of game, we really just need to put ourselves in the situation where **dt is greater than zero**."

## Part 4 (continued): Trading Strategies and Tool Practice

"I talked a bit long on this before, so I'll try to go through it quickly." Evan switched to the next slide.

## Part Three: Will AI Tools Trigger a Hardware Price Increase? An Economics Question

Someone in the discussion raised an interesting question: "If tools like OpenCloud really became widespread, would they cause Mac Minis or other personal computer hardware to see the kind of price spikes we saw when Bitcoin mining drove up graphics card prices?"

### The Real Share of Personal Demand

Steven offered a figure that surprised people: "The demand in the personal market, compared with data centers and the enterprise market, may not even reach 10%."

This is an important realization. We tend to overestimate the influence of the individual consumer market. In reality, **what actually drives hardware prices are the large-scale purchases of enterprise customers.** A single data center's purchase volume can be equal to hundreds of thousands of ordinary consumers.

From the perspective of economies of scale, an increase in demand doesn't necessarily lead to higher prices. When something goes from meeting the needs of 100 people to meeting the needs of 10,000 or 100,000 people, prices may actually fall because of scale effects and supply-chain concentration.

"The most expensive thing is one person's demand. When 100, 10,000, or 100,000 people have the same need, the price actually goes down." Steven explained, "Of course, if the demand has to go through chip computing power or is constrained by the laws of physics, it could be expensive. But if it's only at the software level, or can be met by existing capacity, it's not necessarily expensive, and it may even keep dropping."

### The Truth Behind Memory Price Increases

So why are we seeing memory prices rise? Steven gave a very clear answer: "Micron switched its capacity, originally meant for personal PCs, entirely over to data centers, to big customers like NVIDIA."

That's the real reason for the memory price increase. **It's not that personal demand suddenly exploded, but that the allocation of supply-chain resources changed.** When manufacturers find that selling to data centers earns more money, they naturally adjust their capacity. It's a supply-side story, not a demand-side one.

### Apple's Supply Chain Capability

As for whether the Mac Mini will run short, Steven was optimistic: "Apple's expansion capacity is tied to China's industrial capability. There may be short-term inventory tightness, but it can recover in two weeks at most."

He also made an interesting point: **OpenCloud's barrier to entry is still very high.** You can't expect ordinary people to get everything done with a single sentence. This market is far from reaching "everyone uses it" levels. Most people may not have even heard of this tool, let alone used it.

"So I think the impact on hardware demand is minimal. The bigger force still comes from changes in enterprise and data center demand."

## Part Four: Surfing the Money, an Investment Philosophy from a Coder-Turned-Trader

In the afternoon session, things reached the most exciting part. Evan (Ailun) took the stage to share his experience with US stock investing and quantitative trading. He began with a self-introduction: he graduated with a bachelor's degree in 2019 and originally wanted to be a product manager at Futu, but ended up at Huatai Securities. A year later he found the brokerage job wasn't what he had imagined, so he switched to writing code, working at ByteDance and then Horizon Robotics, and is now at a certain factory. But his main line of work has always been writing: first investment articles, and later technical articles.

"I hope today isn't about teaching everyone how to trade stocks, but about sharing more fundamental logic." Evan said, "Tools change, AI changes, but the way of thinking and the understanding of resource allocation are the things that matter more."

### The Essence of Money: A Resource Dispatch System

Evan shared a concept that changed how he thinks about investing. When he was playing the game *Battlefield*, in the scene where you rob a bank, people shout "Take your vote!" In English, "vote" doesn't just mean a ballot. It also stands for a kind of choice, a kind of "resource dispatch order."

"Money isn't just paper bills. At its core, it's a voucher for resource allocation." Evan said, "Once you build this way of thinking, a lot of things become clear."

He gave an example: we often see news saying "some market evaporated trillions of dollars." Did that money really evaporate? Did that wealth really vanish into thin air?

"It didn't. A crash just means people sold stock and turned the 'goods' into cash. The money wasn't burned. It just moved from one place to another. It may have flowed out of tech and into real estate or bonds. The key is: **do you know where it went?**"

If you're just an ordinary small retail investor, seeing a drop, you can only curse a few times and wait to break even. But if you understand the flow of liquidity, you'll start asking: where is the money going? What's going to rise next?

**Investing is like surfing.** You need to know the direction of the current across the whole pool, and know where the biggest waves are. What you need isn't to wait in place, but to actively look for the places where capital is pouring in.

### Why Choose US Stocks?

Evan spent a long time explaining why he chose to invest in US stocks rather than A-shares or crypto.

"What's the underlying logic behind why the US stock market keeps growing year after year?" He asked the audience. Someone answered that it's because of dollar hegemony and oil settlement: the dollars printed eventually flow back into the US market.

Evan nodded: "That's an important reason. But more fundamentally, **the US is the first choice for the world's wealthy to allocate assets.** As long as you have some money on this planet, as long as you're not completely isolated, your investment advisors and financial planners will definitely tell you to put part of your money into the US market."

What does that mean? It means that every moment, an astronomical amount of capital is flowing into US stocks from all over the world. This market receives an enormous inflow of funds. Whether there's a rate hike or a rate cut, whatever short-term fluctuations there are, the long-term trend is upward.

"If you want to make money, you have to go to the pool with the biggest flow. Without a doubt, that's the US market."

### The Invention of Stocks: The Wisdom of Spreading Risk

To illustrate the value of the stock market, Evan told a historical story.

Stocks were first invented by the Dutch East India Company. In the Age of Discovery, building ships, hiring crews, and buying goods all required huge amounts of capital and carried enormous risk: ships could sink, crews could mutiny, cargo could be seized by pirates. No single merchant could bear that kind of risk alone.

So they invented the stock mechanism: **spread the risk across the whole of society.** You buy a share, I buy a share, and we all share the risk and the returns together.

"The Netherlands had no particular resources and wasn't a big country, but through this financial mechanism, it put the resources of the entire society into the Age of Discovery." Evan said, "And if you had bought the Dutch East India Company's shares back then, you'd have been very happy, because it paid dividends for 200 years."

The story shows that **a stock isn't just a piece of paper. It represents a mechanism of risk distribution and resource allocation.** After several hundred years of evolution, this mechanism has become very mature.

### A Mathematical Model of Growth

Evan offered a simple test for judging growth: "How do you measure whether something is growing continuously? High school math taught us: take the derivative and check whether dt is greater than zero. As long as you're in the positive range, you won't run into major trouble."

What does that mean? It means you should look for tracks where people keep investing and capital keeps flowing in. The AI industry is like that, some energy stocks are like that, and gold is too. They all represent some kind of "scarce resource."

"We're not looking for a zero-sum game, we're looking for a positive-sum game. We want to put ourselves in an environment where dt > 0."

### Strategy in Practice: A QQQ Backtest

Evan showed some backtest data for specific trading strategies. Using the Nasdaq 100 index fund QQQ as an example:

If you had held QQQ from 1993 until now, a 26-year stretch, your return would be about 13 times your money. Sounds good, right? But you may have forgotten that **your maximum drawdown could reach 82%.** That means you might go through a period where your account shrinks by more than 80%. Most people simply can't handle that psychological pressure.

So is there a way to keep a respectable return while lowering the risk? Evan shared a simple strategy: add a moving average filter. When QQQ falls below its 200-day moving average, sell immediately, and buy back once it regains the average.

The return of this strategy drops to about 8 times, but the maximum drawdown is greatly reduced. For most people, this may be a more acceptable risk-return ratio.

"If you want to be more aggressive, you can use 2x or 3x leveraged ETFs." Evan said, "The US stock market has many products like this, where fund companies build in leverage. Of course, returns are higher, but so is the risk."

### Tool Choices: Longbridge and TradingView

Evan specifically recommended two tools.

**First is Longbridge Securities.** "I looked at a lot of brokerages, and Longbridge is the most friendly to individual developers. Its API is very open, so you can easily get historical data and run strategy backtests. In comparison, Futu, despite its big name, isn't as good in this respect."

**Second is TradingView.** "This is a backtesting platform for individuals. You don't need any institutional account. As long as you can write a little Python, you can run your strategy and see how it performed historically."

He also specifically pointed out an MCP (Market Context Protocol) tool that can be called directly within Claude to fetch stock data in real time and generate professional analysis reports.

"But I don't recommend letting AI trade for you directly." Evan said with a smile, "Because every US stock trade has commission fees, around 0.02 percent. If AI traded at high frequency, your fees would be terrifying. AI is good as an adviser, but the actual trading still has to be done by you."

### Cognition and Risk

At the end of the session, Evan shared his core view: "Stock investing is fundamentally about making money through cognition. If you can know the answer before 51% of people do, you can make money. But if you're just following trades and listening to tips, that's dangerous. Why would anyone help you make money? What do they get out of it?"

He also reminded beginners in particular: "If you want to get in, I suggest starting with ETFs, and not going straight for high-risk products like options and futures. Losses are the best teacher, but you have to control the size of your losses and not get liquidated in one go."

Someone in the audience asked a sharp question: "Have you made money from stock trading?"

Evan laughed honestly: "I did make some money last year and the year before. But the big drop at the beginning of this year cost me a lot too. That's the market. Nobody can make money forever. What matters is building the right thinking framework and understanding the logic of capital flows. Tools change, markets change, but these fundamentals don't change."

## Part Five: Building a Really Good Product with AI, a Senior Student's Path as an Indie Developer

The final sharing segment was a senior college student's experience developing an iOS app. He seemed a little nervous, but when he talked about his product, there was light in his eyes.

"Hi everyone, I'm... my handle on Twitter is..." He introduced himself first. "In my spare time I independently developed an iOS app called NeoDB. It's also the first app I've made, and it's now on the App Store, with more than 11,000 users, and over 3,000 stars on GitHub."

The room applauded. For a senior student to get results like this on a first app is no small feat.

### Why Build NeoDB?

"NeoDB is an open-source platform for tracking books, films, and shows, a bit like Douban." He showed the app's interface. "You can mark movies you've watched, books you've read, music you've listened to, and games you've played. You can rate them, write reviews, and see other people's recommendations."

Why did he build it? He gave a very simple answer: "Because I have this need myself."

This echoed a line from the afternoon: "Beautiful yourself is the fastest way to beautiful everyone." Making a product for yourself is the fastest path to making a product for everyone.

"NeoDB itself is a website, but it doesn't have an official mobile app. I'm a user of the website myself, and I wanted to use it on my phone, so I decided to make one. That's the best starting point for a product: starting from your own needs."

### The Complexity of a Product: Beneath the Iceberg

He showed several screenshots of the app: the timeline, the discovery page, the marking page, the settings page, search, user profiles, item details... The dense pages made the audience realize that building an app is far more complex than they imagined.

"These pages are really only 20% of the app." He said something that surprised many people.

"Why? Because for every page you build and every new feature you develop, you have to design several states. **The first is the success state, where the page displays normally. The second is the loading state, where data is still being requested. The third is the error state: network errors, server errors, insufficient user permissions... and this may be subdivided into several types.**"

He went on: "That means every feature needs at least three sets of UI. And that's before counting edge cases: what if the user has no network? What if the user switches accounts? What if data sync fails? All of these have to be considered."

"So I say this is only 20%. The other 80% is the polishing and detail work you don't see."

### Four Stages from Zero to Product

He shared his development process, summarized in four stages:

**Stage one: have an idea.** "The idea doesn't need to be very professional. Many good ideas come from outsiders." He gave an example: he knew an artist friend who, after learning about AI, built an app related to music and art. "If you were just an internet practitioner, you might never have thought of this need."

**Stage two: get the core features running.** "Don't try to make a perfect product right away. My first version was very crude and entirely generated by AI. It had only two functions: logging in, and marking items. But once that core feature worked, the MVP was established."

**Stage three: polish the details.** "This is the most time-consuming stage, maybe 70% of the total development time." He said, "Because AI is very good at building a single page, but making the whole app have a unified design language, smooth animations, and consistent interaction logic requires you to adjust it bit by bit."

He particularly emphasized: **change only one color scheme or add only one animation at a time, not a big overhaul all at once.** "Because AI tends to miss details. If you give it too many instructions at once, it may overwrite features you had before. So you must use Git for version control, so you can roll back at any time."

**Stage four: launch and iterate.** "Once you've completed your first release, the road ahead becomes much clearer. Users will give you feedback, and you'll know what to do next."

### AI Is a Tool, People Are the Core

He spent a long time on one point: **in the AI era, the model doesn't matter as much as the person.**

"Models are updated every day. Yesterday Anthropic released Claude 4.6, OpenAI released 5.3, and DeepSeek released 2.5... Models change, but the people who use them don't. Same model, different people, completely different results. Same person, different models, also different results. So the key is the person."

He gave a vivid example: "AI can help you generate pages, write code, and do design, but what's your product called? What problem does it solve? What style are the colors? How fast should the animation be? These decisions all come from people. **AI is your assistant, but it can't replace you in making decisions.**"

He also mentioned an anxiety that resonates with many people: "I often see people on Xiaohongshu saying 'I made an app in three days,' 'listed on the App Store in two days.' I get anxious and wonder whether spending a month polishing details is too slow."

"But later I realized that products churned out in bulk, like articles mass-generated by AI, don't really have any competitive edge. **Truly good products are always people-led and carefully polished, because products are made for people, and only people know what people need.**"

### Aesthetics Is a Long-Term Practice

Someone asked him: how do you develop product taste?

He admitted honestly: "There's no quick way to build taste. It's a long process of accumulation."

His advice: follow excellent designers, experience carefully designed apps, and observe the small details that seem minor but actually matter, like whether the fonts are aligned, whether you can swipe left to go back, and whether the animation speed is just right.

"I'd recommend following some designers on Twitter and watching interviews with developers of great apps. You don't have to fully understand their technology, but by using those apps, you'll gradually build your own taste."

He also recommended a prototyping tool, Excalidraw: "Its lines are imperfect, and that actually helps you focus on the overall structure instead of getting stuck on pixel-level details. Sometimes I sketch a prototype first, and sometimes I have AI generate one directly and then adjust it."

### Thinking About Monetization

On how to make money, he shared his strategy: "Free download plus in-app purchases. Don't charge for the download, because that would drastically lower conversion."

His approach: make the core features free so users can try them. Then set some advanced features, for example, five free uses per day, with payment required starting from the sixth. "Let users form a habit first, then guide them to pay. The conversion rate is higher that way."

Someone asked how to handle taxes on revenue. He said casually: "Apple automatically handles taxes in each country. You only need to collect the money. For a small individual developer like me, with income not very high yet, you don't need to worry too much about it."

### Done Is Better Than Perfect

Near the end of his talk, he said something very powerful: "You have to finish the product and then launch it. Even if it's not perfect, that's fine."

"I've made some products before, but because of perfectionism, I always felt they weren't good enough, so none of them were ever released. This time I forced myself: finish first, launch first, then iterate slowly. **Once you've taken the first step, the road ahead becomes incredibly clear.**"

"Like today, each of us left home and came to this venue. Once you've taken the first step, you've arrived. If you keep sitting at home thinking 'should I go, what if there's traffic, what if the talk isn't any good,' then you'll never get there."

The room applauded warmly.

## Epilogue: People Are the Most Important Variable

Three hours of sharing came to an end, and outside the window it had already grown dark. Nobody rushed to leave. They gathered together to keep talking, exchanging WeChat contacts and sharing their projects.

The topics of this session were broad: from AI safety to product design, from investment philosophy to indie development, from technical tools to business models. But if you had to find one theme running through the whole session, it would be the line Steven said in his final summary:

"**Beautiful everyone is the fastest way to beautiful no one. Beautiful yourself is the fastest way to beautiful everyone.**"

In an age where AI evolves every day and tools update every day, what truly stays the same? It's human needs, human taste, human judgment, and human creativity.

OpenCloud may be replaced by better tools, QQQ strategies may stop working, and NeoDB may be surpassed by other apps. But if you understand the underlying logic, understand how money flows, understand how products are polished, and understand what users really need, then no matter how the outside world changes, you can find your own place.

AI is a tool, and models change, but people don't. In this fast-changing era, **what's scarcest is not technology but judgment; not tools but ways of thinking; not speed but direction.**

The lights in the café were warm and bright. These young people left with their heads full of ideas and inspiration and walked into the Shanghai night. Some of them may really build a product that changes an industry, some may find their own investing approach in the US market, and some may keep exploring the limits of AI.

But what matters more is that they understood one truth: **in the AI era, people are the most important variable.**
