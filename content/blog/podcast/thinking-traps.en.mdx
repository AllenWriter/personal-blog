Hi everyone, welcome to **Cold-Knowledge Blind Box**.

Today we're talking about a really interesting topic:

**Where do our brains tend to fall into little traps while thinking?**

Put simply, you think you're being rational, but your brain has already been quietly leading you astray.

So let's take apart some of these "pits" in our thinking together.

## **Cognitive Dissonance**

Let's start with the first one, **cognitive dissonance**.

The classic example is the fox. It can't reach the grapes, so it turns around and says:

"Hmph, those grapes are sour."

Why does this happen?

Because there's a conflict between "I want to eat the grapes" and "I can't get the grapes."

To make itself feel better, it changes its mind:

"Actually, I didn't want those grapes anyway."

We do this ourselves all the time.

For example, someone says with their mouth, "Rich people are all greedy," but deep down they really want to become rich.

When that goal turns out to be out of reach, they comfort themselves by putting it down.

The psychologist Leon Festinger said there are three main ways people deal with cognitive dissonance:

- change their behavior;

- change their beliefs;

- or simply distort the facts.

Put simply, it's about making yourself feel comfortable.

But if you rely on it too much, you drift further and further from the truth.

## **The Spotlight Effect**

The second one is the **spotlight effect**.

Here's an example:

You get a little stain on your shirt, and you feel like everyone is staring at it.

A girl goes on a date, her makeup has smudged, and she gets incredibly nervous.

But in reality, other people may not have noticed at all.

After all, everyone is busy caring about themselves, and nobody has time to keep staring at you.

So don't worry too much about other people's opinions.

The world isn't paying that much attention to you. Just relax.

## **Hindsight Bias**

The third one is **hindsight bias**.

As soon as something happens, people very easily say: "I knew it all along!"

For example, when a stock goes up, you say you saw it coming.

When it goes down, you also say you had a feeling about it.

In truth, you didn't really understand anything at the time.

It's just that once the result is out, your brain automatically "edits your memory," making you feel smart.

This kind of illusion is quite dangerous,

because it makes us ignore complexity and think problems are much simpler than they are.

## **Framing Effect**

The fourth one is the **framing effect**.

The same meaning, phrased differently, can produce completely different results.

Take buying popcorn, for example:

- A small bucket is 3 yuan, a large bucket is 7 yuan, and many people will choose the small one.

- But add a "medium bucket for 6.5 yuan," and most people will think:

"Wow, paying 0.5 yuan more for the large bucket is a better deal."

There are also more everyday examples:

- "After we get married, you earn the money and I spend it."

- Rephrased, it becomes:

"After we get married, you take care of earning money to support the family, and I take care of being beautiful."

Doesn't that instantly sound much nicer?

That's the power of the framing effect.

## **The Illusion of Exponential Growth**

The fifth one is **the illusion of exponential growth**.

Here's a brain teaser:

If you fold a sheet of paper in half 50 times, how thick do you think it would be?

Many people would guess 1 meter, or 10 centimeters.

But in fact, its thickness could stretch from the Earth all the way to the Sun!

This is what happens when the brain lacks intuition for exponential growth.

So we often underestimate the power of long-term accumulation.

Whether it's investing or learning, if you keep at it even a little bit,

over a long enough stretch of time, the results can exceed your imagination.

## **The Barnum Effect**

The last one is the **Barnum effect**.

It's when vague descriptions feel accurate no matter who they're applied to.

For example:

"You are a kind person, but sometimes you also feel fragile."

It seems to fit anyone.

That's why horoscopes and personality tests often feel incredibly accurate.

In fact, they just use ambiguous words to let you find yourself in them.

## **Ending**

Alright, today we talked about several thinking traps:

cognitive dissonance, the spotlight effect, hindsight bias, the framing effect, the illusion of exponential growth, and the Barnum effect.

These psychological phenomena remind us:

**Don't trust your brain's first reaction too much, because it often secretly leads you astray.**

Learn to stay alert, learn to adjust, and life becomes a lot easier.
