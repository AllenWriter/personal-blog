First, the planet we move to has to be habitable. That means it can't be too close to the Sun, or it would be scorching hot, and it can't be too far from the Sun, or it would be freezing.

Earth's position is just right, neither too far nor too close. The two planets closest to us are Venus and Mars. Venus has a harsh environment: its air pressure is 92 times that of Earth, its atmosphere is almost entirely carbon dioxide, and it is covered in clouds of dilute sulfuric acid. Its surface temperature stays at around 460 degrees Celsius all year round. Even landing a probe there is extremely hard, and life certainly couldn't survive.

By comparison, Mars's basic conditions are much better. Mars looks red because its surface is made mostly of iron oxide rock, which is very similar to basalt on Earth.

Mars's gravity is about 38 percent of Earth's, and one rotation takes 24.3 hours, which matches the day and night rhythm that humans and most life on Earth are used to. And Mars's axis is tilted a little, so Mars has seasons too, though each season lasts about two years. Put simply, one year is summer and the next is winter.

But Mars's atmosphere is extremely thin. Its air pressure is only one percent of Earth's, and it has no oxygen. This means Mars effectively has no atmosphere to block cosmic rays from outer space, and the ultraviolet light is very strong. The temperature also swings wildly between day and night. On the Martian equator, it can drop to minus 70 degrees at night and climb back to 20 degrees during the day.

And even at its closest approach, Mars is still 55 million kilometers away from Earth. Sending astronauts there takes at least six months, unlike a trip to the Moon, which takes only 8 days and 3 hours. So if we simply sent people to Mars to go around for a day, take a photo, and fly back, the journey would be more symbolic than practical.

If humans really land on Mars, we should do what we did when we built the Antarctic research stations: set up a permanent settlement on Mars in advance. Then, when the next group arrives, the two teams can rotate. That is the more sensible way to explore Mars.

So the first step of the Mars immigration plan has to be transforming Mars's atmosphere. At the very least, we should first build houses on Mars and try growing plants indoors. So then, how did engineers and scientists manage to transcend their era and make the impossible happen? The answer may be surprising, and yes, it's the old method: with no advanced technology, fall back on massed manpower! In an age without artificial intelligence, rely on humans to make intelligence happen!

Take the core Moon-landing computer, for example. The Apollo spacecraft had two computers. One handled taking the command module from Earth to the Moon and back. The other handled calculating the lunar module's trips between lunar orbit and the Moon's surface.

Yes, the geniuses at MIT succeeded in building the smallest, fastest, and most flexible computer of that era. Compared with the ordinary computers that took up half a room, the Apollo computer was only the size of a briefcase, and it was used to help astronauts navigate.

Back then, computers on Earth needed punch cards to run, and results often took hours to come out. It was like clicking a calculate button on your phone and then having to wait hours for the answer.

But the Apollo computer had a keyboard, and it responded immediately. That came down to its unique design. Its memory was core rope memory, which was the densest computer memory of the time. It **had to be woven by experienced female textile workers, who threaded the wires through designated positions with needles**. Every single wire had to be perfectly precise. Yes, just like knitting a sweater: a wire passing through a magnetic ring represented 1, and a wire passing outside the ring represented 0.

Only absolute precision could ensure the program ran properly. Behind the Apollo Moon landing, there were truly many great, ordinary, and overlooked women.

If you've read *The Three-Body Problem*, the idea of a human-array computer shares the same principle as this "woven memory." Both can be called "semiconductors." Because what a semiconductor chip does is essentially process countless 0s and 1s to perform binary operations.

Weaving computers was extremely tedious work, but it required skill, experience, and a great deal of focus.
