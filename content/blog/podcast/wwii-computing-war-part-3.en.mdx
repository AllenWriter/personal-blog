Hi everyone, welcome to Cold-Knowledge Blind Box. In the previous two episodes we covered the prequel to *The Imitation Game*, the story of how Polish codebreakers were the first to take on the German intelligence black box, the Enigma machine, years ahead of Britain and France. Polish mathematicians proposed turning code-breaking into a quantifiable mathematical algorithm problem, which pushed Britain and France into modern intelligence work.

On the eve of the nation's fall, Poland passed this whole secret system against Nazi Germany to Britain and France. In the end it inspired Turing to lead countless women workers at Bletchley Park in building humanity's first computer, laying the foundation for the information revolution.

This time we're focusing on what happened next to the three Polish mathematicians who inspired that pioneering work.

## Ending: Turing Changed the Second Half of the War, the Poles Opened Up the First Half

So if you watch *The Imitation Game* again today, you'll still be moved by Turing.

You'll still think it's a remarkable film.

It's just that you'll know:

it tells the main story.

It is not the whole story.

The prequel that was truly missing is about three young Polish men.

It's about a country on the verge of collapse, which in its final hour handed its most valuable secret to the people who came after.

It's also about the most secret and yet most costly contest in a war: who could first tear open the enemy's nervous system.

If I had to sum up this missing prequel in one sentence, it would be this:

**Turing changed the second half of the war.
The Poles opened up the first half.**

# Epilogue: Where Did the Man Who First Pushed the Door Open End Up?

If this were a standard film, the story would be over by now.

The German black box has been cracked open.

Turing and Bletchley Park took up the torch.

The Allied intelligence machine began to run.

The course of World War II was quietly rewritten.

The camera pulls back, the credits roll, and the audience gets up and leaves.

But the cruelest thing about real history is this:

a film can stop at its most powerful moment,

but a person cannot.

Because when the main feature of *The Imitation Game* begins, the people who actually wrote that prequel did not receive any mythical fate.

Instead, their lives later came to resemble a long film with no audience.

The small team of the Polish Cipher Bureau did not disappear once their country fell.

They did not get wiped off the map with Poland in September 1939.

Instead, they kept working in exile.

In France, they moved into an intelligence station in the Vichy-controlled zone and formed what was called the **Z Group**. They kept breaking Enigma, kept improving their prototype machine, which they called "the bomb," and held on for another two years, grinding it out.

During those two years, they were not reminiscing about past glory.

They were still working.

And not on small matters.

By one count, during this time they broke more than nine thousand German intelligence messages.

These messages directly or indirectly affected German operations in Yugoslavia, Greece and the Soviet Union, and supported the Allied battle plans that later opened the front in North Africa.

In other words, the Polish story did not end with "handing over the baton in 1939 and leaving the stage of history."

Not at all.

Even after losing their country, they kept settling the accounts of this war.

Only now, they no longer had a nation.

They only had their missions.

Then fate began to take them, one by one.

On January 9, 1942, one of the members, **Jerzy Różycki**, died at sea when the ship he was on, returning from Algeria to France, was involved in an accident.

If you pull this detail out on its own, it feels strange.

A person who had helped rewrite the structure of wartime codes did not die in some heroic battle, nor in the kind of thrilling gunfight you'd see in an intelligence film. He died in a maritime accident.

That is the real coldness of history.

It never wraps up its characters the way literature wants.

It doesn't give someone a dignified exit just because they once did something important.

Rejewski and Zygalski did not escape danger because of this, either.

After Germany invaded France, their situation grew more and more dangerous.

Staying meant waiting to die.

So they had no choice but to go into exile again.

On November 9, 1942, the day after the Allies landed in North Africa, they set out on another escape.

By then they were no longer just a few young men studying combinatorics at university.

They were people carrying a huge secret, without a homeland and without a safe rear.

On January 29, 1943, they tried to cross the Pyrenees from France into Spain.

They were caught at the border by Spanish security police and sent to a refugee camp.

It's hard not to picture that scene.

The mathematician who had been the first to tear open a crack in Germany's most secret system,

the man who had made the first move in the code war and changed the fate of an entire hidden front,

was now freezing, falling ill and staying silent in a refugee camp, not daring to reveal his true identity.

Later, Rejewski developed rheumatism.

Throughout all of this, they never revealed to anyone who they really were or what they had done.

That part is especially painful.

Because you realize that many people who truly got things done in history were not recognized by the world right away.

Sometimes it's just the opposite.

While they were alive, the most important part of themselves had to be hidden first. Like Van Gogh. Like Kafka, who entrusted his unpublished manuscripts to a friend but told him to "burn everything." Like Tesla, whose late years were misunderstood by his era and swallowed by commercial narratives. Like the many nameless codebreakers and engineers who took part in decisive work but had to bury their names in archives during the most critical years and leave their résumés blank, because in that world, exposing your identity did not mean winning applause. It was far more likely to mean losing your way out.

Later, they were finally released and made their way to Gibraltar, then sailed to England.

But when they arrived in England, there was none of the "heroes return home" scene so common in literature.

The British, of course, knew what they had done and knew their real role in breaking Enigma.

But they were not pushed back to the center of the stage because of it.

Instead, the British deliberately kept them out of the inner circle.

They were not put back on the main battlefield of Enigma.

They were only asked to analyze a different German cipher system, the SS code.

This arrangement is worth thinking about.

It shows that war has never been simply "whoever contributes most gets the biggest stage."

War is also a combination of systems, organization, secrecy, national interests and the power to control the narrative.

A spark may come from the edge,

but the person standing beside the furnace at the end may not be the one who first lit the fire.

After World War II ended, Rejewski finally returned to Poland.

That was already 1946.

He went back to reunite with his wife and two children.

There was no triumphant homecoming.

No nationwide welcome.

No grand ceremony recognizing that "you changed the course of the war."

He later worked in Poznań.

Some accounts say he did administrative work at a university, and others say he later worked as an ordinary accountant at a factory.

No matter which version is true, the meaning is much the same:

the man who had been among the first to see through the essence of Enigma went on to live an almost ordinary life, so ordinary that the times seemed ready to swallow it.

And he stayed silent for a long time.

About what he had done before and during the war, he said nothing.

He did not talk about it.

He did not explain.

It was as if he had pressed the brightest and heaviest part of his life back into darkness.

In 1967, he retired.

On February 13, 1980, Rejewski died of a heart attack in Warsaw at the age of 75.

He was later buried in a **military cemetery** in Poland, with full military honors.

That is certainly far more fortunate than many people who were completely forgotten.
