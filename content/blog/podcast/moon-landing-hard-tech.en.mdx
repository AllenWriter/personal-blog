So how did the engineers and scientists achieve the impossible, beyond their era? The answer may be surprising, but yes: they relied on the old-fashioned method. With no advanced technology, they used a sea of people! In an era without artificial intelligence, they achieved intelligence by human hands!

Take the core of the entire plan, the Moon landing computer. The Apollo spacecraft had two computers. One was responsible for taking the command module from Earth to the Moon and back. The other was responsible for calculating the round trip between lunar orbit and the lunar surface for the lunar module.

Yes, the brilliant people at MIT managed to build the smallest, fastest and most flexible computer of that era. Compared with the ordinary computers that took up half a room, the computer on the Apollo spacecraft was only the size of a briefcase, used to help astronauts with navigation.

Back then, computers on Earth had to be fed punched cards to run, and results often took hours to come out. It was like clicking a calculate button on your phone and then waiting hours for the answer.

But the computer on the Apollo spacecraft had a keyboard and responded instantly. This came down to its unique design. It used core rope memory, which was the densest miniature computer memory of the time. It had to be woven by experienced female textile workers, who threaded wires through specified positions with needles. Every wire had to be perfectly precise. Yes, just like knitting a sweater: a wire passing through a magnetic ring represented a 1, and a wire passing outside the ring represented a 0.

Only absolute precision could ensure that programs ran properly. Behind the Apollo Moon landing, there were truly many great, ordinary, and overlooked women.

If you've read *The Three-Body Problem*, the idea of the human-array computer shares the same principle as this "woven memory." Both can be called "semiconductors," because what a semiconductor chip does is essentially process countless 0s and 1s to carry out binary operations.

Weaving computers was very dull work, but it required skill, experience, and enough focus.

It's said that the female workers at the factory once staged a brief strike. To keep up with the schedule, the factory manager and foreman sat down and wove themselves, but what they wove turned out to be defective...

Truly, one hour of spaceflight required a million hours of effort from people on Earth.

Apart from weaving computers, textile workers also had to weave the parachutes for the lunar return capsule.

The cloth for these parachutes had to be strong enough to withstand the extremely fast descent of the return capsule, yet light enough. Because even a tiny bit of extra weight could push the lunar module over its weight limit, causing it to miss the proper return trajectory.

Each parachute was made by stitching together pieces of cloth, and one parachute would have 2 million needle holes. Workers stitched every single one by hand on old-fashioned sewing machines. A single wrong stitch could lead to catastrophic consequences.

Finally, these parachutes were folded by workers. During the Apollo missions in the 1960s and 1970s, only three people in the whole United States were certified to fold parachutes for Apollo spacecraft.

Because these three were so incredibly important, NASA forbade them from riding in the same car, in case a car accident killed all of them at once... They really couldn't afford such an accident...
